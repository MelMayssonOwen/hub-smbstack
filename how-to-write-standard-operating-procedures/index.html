How to Write Standard Operating Procedures

Published 2026-10-30

TL;DR: To write a standard operating procedure, pick a process that needs consistency, map the real workflow with the people who do it, then turn that workflow into clear action-first steps with only the details needed to do the job correctly.

Standard operating procedures, or SOPs, are repeatable instructions for a specific task. For a small business, that usually means the same service gets handled the same way, training is less improvisational jazz, and handoffs do not turn into mystery theater.

Why SOPs matter before you start writing

An SOP is a repeatable set of instructions for a specific task. That matters because small businesses often rely on a few people wearing a lot of hats. Consistency is what keeps service, training, and handoffs from drifting every time somebody is out, busy, or new.

Good SOPs also help teams standardize work instead of reinventing it each time. That is the core idea behind them. A process that works once should be written down if it needs to work the same way again.

Decide which process deserves an SOP

Start with the jobs that happen often, cause avoidable errors, touch customers directly, or matter for compliance. Those are the processes most likely to benefit from a written procedure.

Not every task needs formal documentation. If something is rare, simple, or too small to repeat in a meaningful way, a quick note or checklist may be enough. Save the heavier SOP treatment for the work that actually needs consistency.

Gather the people who actually do the work

Talk to the people who perform the task, not just the person who imagines the task from a quiet office chair. That might be employees, managers, or front-desk staff, depending on the workflow.

The goal is to capture the real process, including the little steps people use to keep things moving. If the written version does not match reality, the SOP becomes decorative, which is a fancy way of saying nobody uses it.

Map the task from trigger to finish

Before writing, define the process from start to finish. Identify the trigger that starts the work, the goal of the process, the inputs needed, the tools or systems involved, and the final handoff or completion point.

That scope keeps the SOP from wandering off into unrelated territory. You want a clear beginning, middle, and end, not a document that starts with one task and somehow ends up explaining office politics.

Write the SOP in plain, action-first language

Turn the workflow into short, direct steps. Use verbs first, keep the wording clear, and avoid vague instructions like “handle it appropriately” unless you enjoy future confusion.

Spell out what done looks like at each stage. If a step is complete when a form is saved, a call is logged, or a confirmation is sent, say so. The reader should not need to guess whether they are finished or merely emotionally committed.

Include the details someone needs to do the job correctly

Add the details that matter for performance and consistency. That can include:

  • Roles and responsibilities
  • Required approvals
  • Forms or records to complete
  • System fields that must be entered
  • Exceptions and edge cases
  • Safety or compliance notes
  • Links to related documents or policies

Do not overload the SOP with trivia. Include the information someone needs to do the job correctly, then stop before it turns into a novel with office software.

Add a worked example: a small-business appointment scheduling SOP

Here is a concise example for a front-desk or service team.

Task: Handle a new appointment request

  1. Answer the request by phone, email, or booking form.
  2. Check the calendar for open time slots that match the service and staff availability.
  3. Confirm the appointment time with the customer.
  4. Enter the booking details into the scheduling system, including customer name, contact information, service type, date, and time.
  5. Send the confirmation message to the customer.
  6. Log any special notes, such as access instructions, service preferences, or follow-up needs.
  7. Notify the assigned staff member, if your workflow requires it.
  8. Close the task when the booking is saved and the confirmation is sent.

This is the difference between a useful SOP and a vague one. It shows the actual flow, the handoff, and the finish line.

Review, test, and revise the SOP with the team

Once the draft is written, have the people who will use it follow it once. Watch for unclear steps, missing details, or places where the SOP does not match how the work actually happens.

Then revise it. This is the unglamorous part, but it is where the SOP becomes usable instead of theoretical.

Store it where staff will actually use it and keep it current

Publish the SOP in a shared location that staff can find without a scavenger hunt. If people cannot reach it quickly, they will default to memory, and memory is a quirky little system.

Assign ownership for updates so someone is responsible when software, policy, or workflow changes. Review SOPs whenever the process changes enough to make the old version inaccurate.

FAQ: Do SOPs need to be long?

An SOP should be as long as needed to make the task repeatable, and no longer. If a short procedure does the job, great. If the task has approvals, exceptions, or compliance steps, it may need more detail.

FAQ: What should I write first, the steps or the formatting?

Write the workflow first. After that, format it into headings, numbered steps, and notes that match how staff will use it.
